01 — Overview

Four Very Different Communities

American Jewry is not a monolith. The median age alone spans 27 years between Orthodox (35) and Conservative (62) — and the differences only deepen from there.

What this means for the future: Orthodox Jews are just 9% of U.S. Jewish adults today — but their median age of 35 and 3.3 children per adult means they are a far larger share of Jewish children. Conservative Jewry has the oldest median age (62) and the smallest household size, pointing to structural demographic decline.

02 — Practice & Observance

What American Jews Actually Do

Religious practice varies enormously. Even culturally iconic holidays like the Passover Seder show a 60-point gap between Orthodox (97%) and unaffiliated Jews (37%).

% Who Observe Each Practice

The Seder as cultural glue: At 67%, even Reform Jews hold Passover Seders at high rates. The Seder functions less as religious obligation and more as a cultural anchor for non-Orthodox Jews.
Kosher as boundary marker: Keeping kosher at home (Orthodox 95%, Reform 5%) is the single sharpest practice divide — a 90-point gap that maps almost perfectly onto Orthodox vs. non-Orthodox lines.

03 — Jewish Identity

What Makes Someone Jewish?

Across all measures of Jewish attachment, Orthodox Jews cluster near 100% while unaffiliated Jews cluster near 15–20%. The two groups are working with fundamentally different definitions of Jewishness.

% With Strong Jewish Identification

What Is "Essential" to Being Jewish? — All U.S. Jews

Law vs. ethics: 76% of all Jews say remembering the Holocaust is essential — the highest-ranked item. Observing halakha ranks last at 15%. But among Orthodox Jews, 83% call halakha essential. These aren't just different priorities — they represent divergent conceptions of what Judaism fundamentally is.

04 — Community & Connectedness

How Connected Are American Jews?

Orthodox Jews live in dense, highly Jewish social worlds — 88% say all or most of their close friends are Jewish. For Reform and unaffiliated Jews, Jewish social ties are far more diffuse.

05 — The Continuity Question

Is American Judaism Declining — or Just Changing?

Intermarriage has dramatically accelerated. Orthodox communities are growing younger and larger while liberal denominations face steeper demographic headwinds.

Intermarriage Over Time — % with Non-Jewish Spouse

Average Children Per Adult

Orthodox fertility: 3.3 children per adult vs. 1.4 for Reform. Combined with median age 35, Orthodox Jews are the fastest-growing segment of American Jewry.

Continuity Indicators

Intermarriage by denomination: 98% of married Orthodox Jews have a Jewish spouse. Among non-Orthodox Jews married since 2010, 72% married a non-Jewish spouse.
Raising children Jewish: Among intermarried couples, only 28% are raising children as Jewish by religion. 30% are raising them in no religion at all.

06 — Politics

Overwhelmingly Democratic — Except Orthodox

American Jews are one of the most reliably Democratic constituencies: 71% lean Democratic. But Orthodox Jews break sharply — 75% identify as Republican, mirroring white evangelical alignment.

Party Identification

Political Ideology

Jews in Context — % Liberal & % Democrat Across U.S. Religious Groups

Where Jews sit in the American religious landscape: Reform Jews (80% Democrat, 55% Liberal) are among the most left-leaning religious groups in America. Orthodox Jews (20% Democrat, 9% Liberal) sit closer to white evangelical Christians — whose 82% Republican alignment makes them the most reliably conservative religious bloc. Evangelical and Catholic comparison data is from separate Pew religion and politics surveys.
The Orthodox exception: 75% of Orthodox Jews identify as Republican. Trump received 81% approval among Orthodox Jews (vs. 18% among Reform). This reflects Orthodox priorities around Israel, religious school funding, and social conservatism — a fundamentally different Jewish identity model.

07 — Jews by Religion vs. No Religion

Two Very Different Jewish Worlds

Pew divides Jewish Americans into "Jews by religion" (73%) — those who say their religion is Jewish — and "Jews of no religion" (27%), who identify Jewishly by culture or ancestry but not faith. The behavioral and attitudinal gaps are enormous.

The religious/secular divide: "Jews of no religion" are not simply less observant — they represent a fundamentally different form of Jewish identity rooted in culture and ancestry. Their children are far less likely to identify as Jewish at all, which is why this 27% share of U.S. Jewish adults sits at the center of the continuity debate.

08 — Generations & Demographics

An Aging Movement — Except Orthodox

Jewish America's generational profile reveals denominations on divergent trajectories. Reform and Conservative Jews skew dramatically older while Orthodox Jews have the youngest age profile of any denomination — a gap with compounding long-term consequences.

The education gap: 60% of Jewish Americans hold a college degree vs. 30% of U.S. adults; 28% hold a postgraduate degree vs. 11% nationally. This multi-generation educational overrepresentation shapes Jewish political alignment, urban concentration, and economic profile — distinguishing American Jewry from virtually every other U.S. religious community.

09 — Israel & Jewish Solidarity

A Divided Relationship with Israel

Views on Israel diverge sharply by denomination and by politics. Orthodox Jews feel the strongest connection to Israeli Jews; Reform and unaffiliated Jews feel much less. Meanwhile, non-Jewish Republicans' support for Israel does not necessarily extend to favorable views of American Jews.

% Feeling "A Lot in Common" with Israeli Jews — by Denomination & Political Lean

Non-Jewish Americans: Favorable Views Toward Israel vs. Toward U.S. Jews

Orthodox–Reform gulf: 67% of Orthodox Jews feel "a lot in common" with Israeli Jews; only 12% of Reform Jews say the same. 60% of Reform Jews report feeling "little or nothing in common" with Orthodox Jews — a split running through the heart of U.S. Jewish communal life.
Philo-Semitism without solidarity: Non-Jewish Trump supporters rate Israel favorably (63%) at a higher rate than they rate U.S. Jews favorably (31%). Christian Zionism drives pro-Israel sentiment independently of views about Jewish Americans themselves.

10 — Cultural Life

Judaism Through Culture and Objects

For many American Jews — especially the unaffiliated — Judaism is primarily a cultural identity. Most own Jewish ritual objects and engage with Jewish content, even without regular synagogue attendance or formal religious practice.
